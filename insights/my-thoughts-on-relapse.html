My thoughts on relapse

September 5, 2026

I had almost three good months this year. No flare-ups, no spiral of anxious thoughts every time I felt a twinge in my back. I'd started to talk about that stretch in the past tense, like something I'd finished, the way you'd describe a course you completed. Then one ordinary Tuesday, I bent down to pick something up off the floor and everything came back at once, the pain, and right behind it, the old familiar dread.

I've been sitting with that word, relapse, ever since. It's a heavy one. It implies you'd fixed something, and now you haven't. I don't think that's quite right, but I understand why it's the word that comes to mind.

What I used to believe about setbacks

For a long time I treated any return of symptoms as proof that nothing I'd done had actually worked. If the pain came back, the stretching hadn't worked. If the anxiety spiked again, then I hadn't really gotten better at managing it, I'd just been lucky for a while. That belief made every good stretch feel fragile, like I was waiting for the other shoe to drop rather than actually living in it.

Looking back, that mindset made the setbacks worse than they needed to be. The physical flare-up was one problem. The story I told myself about what it meant, that I was back at square one, that the good months didn't count, was a second problem stacked directly on top of the first.

What I think relapse actually is

I've come to think of it less like falling off a cliff and more like weather. A good stretch doesn't mean the underlying condition is gone, it means the conditions were favorable for a while. Bodies that have dealt with chronic pain, and minds that have dealt with chronic anxiety, don't usually get a clean, permanent fix. They get better management, longer stretches of calm, faster recoveries when something does flare up. That's real progress, even if it isn't the finish line I used to imagine.

The three months weren't erased by the Tuesday that undid them. They were real. This flare-up is also real. Both things are true at once, and I don't think I have to pick one to believe.

What's different this time

The pain itself doesn't feel that different from previous flare-ups. What's different is how quickly I recognized what was happening in my head, the anxiety trying to convince me the last three months hadn't mattered. I noticed the thought instead of just believing it. That's not nothing. It's not the same as not having the setback at all, but it's a genuinely different relationship to it than I had a year ago.

I also didn't wait as long to go back to the things that actually help, the stretches, the short walks, being honest with the people around me about what I'm dealing with instead of quietly disappearing into it. Last time it took me two weeks to do that. This time it took two days.

What I'd say to someone in the middle of one

If you're in a flare-up right now, physical or mental, and it feels like it's undoing everything you worked for, I don't think that's true, even though I know exactly how convincing it feels in the moment. The setback is real. So was the progress that came before it. Measuring recovery by whether the bad days ever come back sets an impossible bar. Measuring it by how you respond when they do is a fairer, and honestly more accurate, way to see it.

I'm still in it as I write this, if I'm honest. But I know what tends to help, and I'm doing those things instead of waiting for the flare-up to pass on its own. That's the whole shift, really. Not avoiding relapse. Getting quicker and steadier at meeting it.

I mentioned this piece to Dr. Asad Ismail of Sunrise Recovery while writing it, and his framing has stuck with me since:

"Relapse doesn't erase your progress, but it is a signal that something needs attention. Recovery is about recognizing the warning signs, asking for help, and finding the strength to begin again."

— Dr. Asad Ismail, Sunrise Recovery
